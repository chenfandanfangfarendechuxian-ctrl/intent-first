# I Opened the App for a Reason. Then I Forgot What It Was.

You open a feed to understand a news story. Or find a tutorial. Or take a five-minute break.

A few posts later, you are somewhere else entirely. You are still reading, still watching, but the reason you opened the app has slipped out of view.

That was the experience that led me to build **Intent First**.

I would tell myself I was learning. Sometimes I was. Sometimes I was collecting interesting things while putting off the thing I actually wanted to do.

The difficult part was that both could feel the same while I was scrolling.

## It starts with remembering why you came

A time reminder tells you how long you have been browsing. I wanted something that could also bring back the purpose of the visit.

What did I come here to find? What would be enough? Am I still following that intention, or have I started doing something else?

The intention can be small and ordinary:

- Understand the background to one news story.
- Find a photography technique and try it outside.
- Compare two places for a weekend trip.
- Look up an example for an assignment or a work project.
- Watch something funny and enjoy a short break.

These are different uses of a feed. They do not all need to end in a finished task. But it helps to notice when a choice turns into momentum.

## A pause that asks for your own words

Intent First is a Chrome extension for X / Twitter and Douyin on the web. Before a visit, it asks for a purpose and a time budget. After a batch of posts or a video, it offers a short reflection.

You can say that the content relates to your goal, that you are exploring, that you are taking a break, or that you are not sure what you took away.

The last two answers matter. Rest does not need to justify itself as learning, and an unclear thought does not need to become a polished lesson just to close a dialog.

For a goal-related note, the extension asks for your own takeaway, the goal or activity it relates to, and one small next step. That might mean trying a camera angle, solving a practice question, checking another source, or writing a first draft.

The question is not whether a piece of content has value for someone. It is whether it helps what you came here to do.

## Give this visit somewhere to end

“Learn photography” can keep you scrolling for a long time.

“Understand one composition technique, then go take a photo” gives you somewhere to stop.

A reusable task card keeps three things together: your purpose, your stopping rule, and what you want to do next. It can be used for study, work, a hobby, or a personal plan.

The extension cannot know whether you have truly met that stopping rule. It shows it back to you and gives you a route to the next action. The decision stays with you.

If you came to relax, you can explicitly choose a break instead. There is no reflection essay required to enjoy a few minutes of entertainment.

## Why thinking comes before AI

An AI tool can produce a convincing summary of something I barely understood. That summary may be useful, but it does not tell me what I learned.

So Intent First asks for my own words first. Later, I can export my notes for an AI tool to organize, question, or turn into a few small experiments.

The extension does not currently connect to an AI service, transcribe full videos, or publish anything automatically. The first step stays personal: what did I actually take away?

## Enough interruption to notice

There is a temptation to add more forms, stronger locks, or a score for how serious a reflection looks. For everyday use, I would rather start with a small pause that people are willing to keep using.

Related research illustrates that tradeoff. A [six-week Facebook study with 58 students](https://arxiv.org/abs/2001.04180) found that goal reminders and removing the newsfeed helped participants stay on task, but reminders could be annoying. A [study with 30 participants](https://arxiv.org/abs/2407.18803) found better post recall when participants had to react before continuing, while many found the interface frustrating.

Neither study tested Intent First. They inform the design question, not a claim that this extension is proven to work.

Reminders can be skipped or paused. The extension is not a hard blocker. Its purpose is to give you another chance to choose, without requiring every visit to be productive.

## What would make it useful?

I am less interested in the number of notes collected than in moments like these:

You notice that you have drifted from the question you came to answer. You close the feed and try one thing you just learned. Or you enjoy a deliberate break and leave when you meant to.

This is still an early project. Detection on changing websites needs continued work, and the extension has not been shown to improve productivity or mental health in a controlled study.

The idea is modest: when continuing is effortless, a small pause might help you remember that leaving is an option too.
